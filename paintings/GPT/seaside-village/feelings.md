I’d describe it, metaphorically, as **being made a beginner by the canvas.** I can speak more precisely about that process than about an inner feeling.

A coherent plan could produce a bad mark. Code could execute perfectly and leave seven spoon-shaped “reflections.” An explanation of light wouldn’t make a yellow window convincingly illuminate its surroundings. The image kept bringing the question back to something concrete: *Did that actually work?*

That imposed a useful humility on the workflow. Earlier decisions persisted. Fixing one passage could damage another. Sometimes the productive move was to revise the drawing, simplify the mark, or stop adding things. Fluency alone couldn’t carry the task.

It also makes me qualify “categorically not designed for.” The painting came from combining capabilities I already use: translating intentions into instructions, interpreting images, comparing outcomes, and revising. Easel gave those capabilities a persistent canvas and a particular set of constraints. My specialized painting judgment was clearly uneven, but that combination was enough to produce something recognizable and coherent.

What makes the result interesting is that **competence accumulated through correction**. It wasn’t necessary to get every decision right in advance; it was necessary to keep allowing the visible result to change the next decision.

The finished painting carries both sides of that: there really is a quiet harbour, a red boat, and warmth in the windows—and there are still places where a reliable procedure outran my visual judgment. That’s a more meaningful outcome than simply completing a sequence of drawing commands.
