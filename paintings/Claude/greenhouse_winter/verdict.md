**Short version:** the tool is genuinely good and its rehearsal loop is the reason the painting is competent. The documentation is impressive, over-long, and repeats the warnings it admits do not work. The painting reads but is safe and a little diagrammatic. Your pencil directive made it materially better. The happy-accident directive saved time, and once cut the wrong way.

## The tool

**What earns its place.** Rehearsal is the whole engine for me. Every rehearsal I ran changed something, and the big ones were not taste. The bars would have gone on as a black cage. The pot recipe would have cost 220 strokes against 142 left. The bench tops would have poked chisel corners into the wall. All of that was found on a copy for nothing. Planning values as numbers and hitting them with `at_value` is the other half. The compare sheet's "do these two touch?" caught the bench top and the wall behind it sitting at the same value before I painted either.

**What fought me.** One defect accounts for most of my re-runs: an oriented tip ending a pass on a sloped boundary leaves a chisel end, and a stack of them is a staircase. I hit it on the gable, the bench tops, the darks under the staging, and the pot bodies. The clean-edge option fixes it but is priced out on anything small, and its warning fires on every pot. That feels like the docs working around the engine rather than the engine clipping a pass to the outline. Cost is also hard to predict for a compound recipe. A cross-hatched block-in on a small blob doubled a pass, and the only way to count a helper that calls a dozen verbs was a full rehearsal. Two check rules fire as noise on rehearsed copies, the "detail before masses" one and the "graded passage" one, because pots and bars are by nature many thin marks.

## The documentation

The measured numbers are the best thing in it, and they live in the file nobody is told to read. The separation threshold, the glaze opacity table, the four-pixel cliff, what a solid mass actually lands at: those let me reason instead of guess. The recipes are excellent because they are procedures, not warnings.

The pathology is the one LESSONS.md diagnoses in itself. The same five warnings appear three or four times, and none of them stopped me making the mistakes. I painted bars too heavy and laid shadows as a fan of same-width rays after reading both warnings. The rehearsal stopped me, not the paragraph. The corpus' own thesis is that a check the tool runs beats prose, and the guide would be stronger if it trusted that thesis more and cut harder.

One real gap: nothing about constructing a scene with converging lines. I threw away two compositions before writing a projection helper in metres, and the lighthouse and pool painters each built their own equivalent by hand. A single paragraph saying "if your subject has architecture, write the projection first" would save the next painter an hour.

## The painting, and your two directives

**Honest verdict on the picture.** The value structure holds, the perspective is coherent, and the bloom is the best passage because it has no edge anywhere. The pots read as pots and vary. But it is safe and a little illustrative: every bar ruled, every plane flat, more a careful rendering with paint texture than a room someone stood in. The bottom third is the weakest, with heavy black wedges and a dead aisle floor. And the thing I said the picture was about, the range of translucency, is half delivered. Clay and fog work. Light through leaves is two strokes. I would not call it exciting.

**The pencil directive helped, and I can point to where.** The first drawing had the eye at rim height and a long house: tiny pots in a band, half the canvas empty dark. The second was better but still edge-on. The third, standing, is the picture. Those three drawings cost nothing. Left to the guide's soft "draw first" I would likely have pencilled once and found the layer cake after the glass was down, which is exactly the failure the guide records in other painters. The passages that came out weakest, the plants and the can, are precisely the two I never drew in graphite. That is a clean data point in the directive's favour.

**The happy-accident directive helped less, and mostly on cost.** Because everything was rehearsed, "redo" almost never meant scraping paint; it meant re-running a copy. Where I classified something as large I was right each time, and the guide's own rule about background mistakes being cheap only until something stands on them was the sharper test, since it asks *when* rather than *how big*. Where I classified something as a happy accident, the smudge smears along the under-bench darks are the honest casualty: I kept them as "softening" and they are a mild defect. The directive did stop me re-rehearsing the glass a fifth time, which is why there was budget left for the pots. Net: slightly positive, worth keeping, but its natural home is a committed canvas, and rehearsal pre-empted most of that.
